1. What the rest of the course is for

The first half of the semester built tools: sampling distributions, unbiasedness, variance, confidence intervals, power and sample size. The second half uses them for causal inference. The ending should leave students able to read the published studies behind their final projects and to judge each one’s design and claims with tools they already have. They should finish seeing how much better placed they are to understand the world than they were in September.

Several points carry the remaining classes.

  1. Power, told through the NSW job-training study. NSW was too small. Its randomized comparison is valid, but it can’t tell large effects from small ones. A study that is priced wrong yields little even when it is large and important. A significant result from a study like that is also untrustworthy, which is where Type M and Type S errors come in. Those errors are one small addition; the main lesson is to design studies properly and to know what goes wrong when people don’t. Students were promised this point in the Georgia power lecture.
  2. Knowing what is random. Model-based calculations under sampling with replacement or complete randomization are conservative. With a finite population you can always buy precision, because in the limit you ask everyone. A treatment effect gives you no such option: everyone still shows only one of their two outcomes. Homework 6’s small-county exercise already carries the finite-population half of this. The with-replacement formula asks for more calls than the county has registered voters, and the without-replacement formula gives a feasible design.
  3. Sampling followed by randomization, as an interpretive frame. It is rarely used as a design. It is the missing step for reading two kinds of claims: what an experiment on a finite group says about a larger population, and when an observational comparison can be read as causal.
  4. Observational studies. Confounding, matching (after matching, the analysis is still a comparison of two means), and Cornfield’s smoking argument for what matching cannot fix. Leaving covariate adjustment to next semester does not remove this causal material.
  5. Final projects. The final project replaces Exam 2, at 30% of the grade. There is no final exam and the exam block is not used. Late-semester class time goes to project work.

3.2 Sampling, then randomization: identification and what results mean (one class; proposed Nov 19)

What it is for. Combining random sampling with randomization is rarely used as a design. It is the frame for reading two kinds of claims students will meet in their project papers: - what an experiment on a finite group of people says beyond those people; - when a comparison of groups nobody randomized can be read as causal.

The identification argument is the core. If we sample people from a population and then randomize them, independently of everything else, the observed group mean estimates the population mean of that potential outcome: \frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^m y_j(w) = \E\{Y_i(w)\} = \E\{Y_i(w) \mid W_i = w\} = \E\{Y_i(W_i) \mid W_i = w\} = \E(Y_i \mid W_i = w). - The first equality uses the sampling design. - The second uses only the independence of assignment, and it is where randomization enters. - The third is the indicator trick, and the fourth is the definition of Y_i.

The second step is the one that fails when w_j is a property of the person. That is Tuesday’s dark-chocolate point, now in general.

Source. Skip’s 2024 Lecture10, “Potential Outcomes and Causality”, sections “Combining Randomization and Sampling” through “Causal Identification” and “Unbiasedness”. It is set up with complications this class doesn’t need: a campaign-donation example, an age covariate on every plot, and coin-flip assignment. None of them is essential to the identification argument. The coin flip matters only in the direct unbiasedness proof, which is simpler with fixed group sizes.

How to teach it from the 2024 slides. - “The Idea”, “Randomization and Sampling” and “What Happens to (Not Within-Group) Means”: show them as they are. Say “ignore age; the horizontal position is just spread”, and read “flips a coin” as “is assigned”. - “What Happens to the Means”: this one is about within-age-group means. Skip it. - “Sampling and Randomization”: show the two-stage process. Ignore the x_j column. Its six-person table is not this semester’s six people. - “Causal Identification”: show it as it is. It is the class. - “Unbiasedness”: with fixed n_w the random count N_w is a constant, so the last step of the proof is trivial. Add one line: by linearity, \E(\hat\tau) = \bar\tau. - Don’t use the shortened version cut from Tuesday’s deck. It dropped the identification result.

3.3 Observational studies and causality (one class; proposed Dec 1)

What it is for. Leaving covariate adjustment to next semester does not remove the causal work. Students should leave able to say when a comparison of groups nobody randomized can be read as causal, and how far a failure of that could go. The content: - The idea of confounding. Treatment status carries information about what would have happened without treatment. In the identification argument, that is the independence step failing. - Matching. After matching, the analysis is still a comparison of two means; the covariates are used only to choose the comparison. Students saw matching and imbalance in the power deck: the NSW/CPS comparison differed in marriage, 51.4% married among CPS peers against 18.9% of NSW treated men, and that alone moved the benchmark. - Cornfield and smoking. It is a sensitivity argument: how strongly would an unmeasured confounder have to be tied to smoking to explain away the lung-cancer association? It shows what matching can’t fix, and why a strong observational result can still be convincing. - An option under consideration, not decided: a single-covariate example. Compare within the covariate’s groups, then ask what assumption makes those comparisons causal. It would pick up the marriage imbalance above.

Sources. - There is no deck for this class. - The observational studies chapter (linked in 3.7) is a draft written by an agent in September. Skip never reviewed it. It builds a simulated observational study from the Michigan household data, defines confounding, and then compares like with like within covariate groups. That last step goes further into adjustment than this semester intends. - Skip’s 2024 notes for this meeting (not posted) have short sections on randomizing as a function of covariates, the pitfalls of treating observational studies as randomized, and a placeholder for a sensitivity analysis. - The Cornfield material will need to be prepared. The instructor can draw on Cornfield et al. (1959), “Smoking and Lung Cancer: Recent Evidence and a Discussion of Some Questions”.

3.4 Models (instructor notes, no deck)

No finished Models lecture exists, and none should be presented as finished. The course’s Models chapter draft is about regression models. It belongs to next semester and does not make this argument. The notes below are the argument Skip intended. Use them for part of a class (proposed Dec 3) and as the theme of Homework 7.

The argument. - People abstract their data into models, for example “an independent sample with replacement”. The question is what such a model means, and whether it applies here. What does independence mean, and where does it come from in this study? - In the two designs students know best, sampling with replacement and complete randomization, model-based calculations are conservative: they don’t understate the uncertainty. - The finite-population case. If the model says you are in trouble, look closer. You can always get as precise an estimate as you want of a finite population’s mean, because in the limit you ask everyone. A model that treats the population as infinite hides that option. Homework 6’s small-county exercise is the example. The with-replacement formula asks for more calls than the county has registered voters; the without-replacement formula gives a feasible design. - The treatment-effect case. Here the conservativeness remains, but the escape is gone. Even if you include everyone, each person shows only one of their two outcomes. The variance term for how much effects vary across people can’t be estimated, so the conservative standard error is the best available. This is Nov 10’s “What We Can Estimate and What We Cannot”. - The bridge to next semester. Next semester works with regression models directly. The designs from this semester are the reference point: a model earns its uncertainty statements by reproducing the part of a credible design that the question needs.

3.6 Final projects (proposed Dec 3 for part of the class, and Dec 8)

What is settled. - The final project replaces Exam 2, at 30% of the grade. - There is no exam block. - Students work in groups of two or three on one of six published-research topics. The topics page is linked below. - The project is about reading real studies with the tools from this course. What were the researchers trying to learn? What did they measure, sample or randomize? Is the uncertainty right for how the data arose? How could the study have been designed better? What does simulation show? Students are not required to find errors; a careful finding that the analysis is sound is a good result.

What is not set. Deliverables, due dates and the presentation format have not been decided. Project discussions and presentations take place in regular class meetings. The proposed arrangement uses part of Dec 3 and all of Dec 8, plus Friday sessions for project work.

Where the course connects. Each topic pairs with something from the second half of the course: - design and power with the NSW story; - the unit of randomization and the dependence it creates, as with Michigan households and the vaccination-camp study; - what was randomized against what was merely observed, with 3.2 and 3.3; - whether the measured outcome is the thing we care about.

That connection, applied to a real paper, is the ending: students see how much they can now read and judge.
