Change the Conventions

  • Back to two-sided intervals. We can change the standard of proof, and we can change how much failure risk we’re willing to tolerate.
  • People describe these as two requirements. In the picture, they add up to one distance in standard errors.
  • Everything here is measured in standard errors. Changing units doesn’t change the geometry.
  • 95% coverage and 80% power: 1.96 + 0.84 = 2.80 standard errors.
  • 99% coverage and 80% power: push the dashed cutoff out.
  • 2.58 + 0.84 = 3.42 standard errors.
  • 95% coverage and 90% power: tolerate only 10% missed funding.
  • 1.96 + 1.28 = 3.24 standard errors.

For the same expected earnings and standard, a bigger required distance in SE units means a smaller standard error—and more data.

These are planning conventions, including in clinical-trial guidance (FDA/ICH E9, §3.5).

Power From Bias

What people don’t talk about enough is the power you get from bias. Not like, metaphorically.

  • The green line is just a line.
  • Coverage; power; they’re all just counting how many intervals cross a line. One is the complement of the other.
  • The more bias you have, the more power you have against the truth.
  • Coverage is just lack of power to reject the truth.
  • You thought this was about the relationship between the green line and the orange one.
    • Nope. It’s about the relationship between the blue line and any other line.
    • Blue was there the whole time: the mean of the estimator’s sampling distribution.
  • One way of thinking about this is to completely flip the picture. If you sample from the CPS population instead of the NSW one, your sampling distribution is centered on our friend the orange line.
    • You’re at the wrong line entirely.
    • What would’ve been 80% power if you were trying not to estimate the green line…
    • …is 20% coverage because you are.
  • Before, about 2.8 standard errors separated the truth from the standard we wanted to rule out. Now the same distance separates the estimator’s centre from the truth. Same geometry, different labels.

\underset{\text{coverage}}{\Pr(\text{covers green})} \;=\; 1-\underset{\text{power}}{\Pr(\text{doesn't cover green})}

n=501: 80% exclude green; 20% cover green.

n=1002: 98% exclude green; 2% cover green.

n=2004: 99.99% exclude green; 0.01% cover green.

Reality and What We’re Doing

  • What we have is a point estimate and an estimated sampling distribution that tells us a width.
  • The thing we want to know is the one thing we never see. An interval says: we think it’s somewhere in here.
  • With an unbiased estimator, we have a theoretical reason to center that interval where we do. Bias can take that reason away.

What we construct from the study is the estimate and its width. The standard is something we chose.

  • The distribution our estimate came from, where it is centred, and where the truth sits are all in this picture and none of them come with the data.
  • We drew them in to reason about them. The study hands us one estimate and a width.

If a line is right, we want intervals to contain it. If it is wrong, we want intervals to exclude it.

We just gave ourselves power against the truth.

\text{coverage}=1-\Pr(\text{interval excludes the truth}).

Calculating Coverage

Set Up

  • Return to n=30. Above, I’ve drawn the sampling distribution for the estimator \hat\mu and a normal approximation to it.
    • It is centred at \tilde\mu = 7,103 and the truth is \mu = 4,555, so the bias is 2,548.
  • We’re going to calculate that estimator’s coverage rate. Approximately. We’ll make some simplifications.
  • We’ll act as if the normal approximation to the sampling distribution were perfect.
  • We’ll assume our intervals are perfectly calibrated.
    • i.e., for a draw \hat\mu from the sampling distribution …
    • the interval is \hat\mu \pm 1.96\sigma where \sigma is our estimator’s actual standard error.

Which Estimates Hit The Target?

Let’s think about things from the target’s perspective. The solid green line.

Give it arms the same length as our intervals have: out to the dotted green lines.

Which point estimates produce intervals that hit the target?

An interval’s arms reach the target if and only if the target’s arms reach that interval’s center.

We integrate the sampling distribution’s density between the target’s arms.

\text{coverage} = \int_{\mu-1.96\sigma}^{\mu+1.96\sigma} f(x) dx \qqtext{ where } f(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi} \sigma} e^{-\frac{(x-\tilde\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}

Everything in Standard Errors

Earnings units. Measure position in dollars.

Standard-error units. Measure from blue, in units of one standard error.

u=\frac{x-\tilde\mu}{\sigma},\qquad \text{green is at }-\frac{\tilde\mu-\mu}{\sigma} =-\frac{\text{bias}}{\text{se}}.

Same picture. Same shaded probability. A different ruler.

Five Minutes

Rewrite this probability in standard-error units:

\int_{\mu-1.96\sigma}^{\mu+1.96\sigma} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma} e^{-\frac{(x-\tilde\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\,dx.

Use u=(x-\tilde\mu)/\sigma. Write down the new endpoints, the new exponent, and what happens to dx.

It’s conceptually simple and a little finicky. Try it; then we’ll look at the solution.

A Standard Normal Area

You struggled, didn’t you? Me too. Here’s the solution.

Make the substitution u=(x-\tilde\mu)/\sigma. Then x=u\sigma+\tilde\mu and dx=\sigma\,du.

What happens to the endpoints?

As x goes from \mu-1.96\sigma to \mu+1.96\sigma, u goes from -1.96-(\tilde\mu-\mu)/\sigma to 1.96-(\tilde\mu-\mu)/\sigma.

\begin{aligned} \text{coverage} &= \int_{\mu-1.96\sigma}^{\mu+1.96\sigma} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi} \sigma} e^{-\frac{(x-\tilde\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}} dx \\ &= \int_{-1.96-(\tilde\mu-\mu)/\sigma}^{1.96-(\tilde\mu-\mu)/\sigma} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi} \sigma} e^{-\frac{u^2}{2}} \sigma du \\ &= \int_{-1.96-(\tilde\mu-\mu)/\sigma}^{1.96-(\tilde\mu-\mu)/\sigma} \textcolor{blue}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{u^2}{2}}} du \end{aligned}

Summary and Generalization

  • We can calculate the coverage of a biased, approximately normal estimator easily.
  • It’s an integral of the standard normal density over the range \pm 1.96 -\text{bias}/\text{se}.
  • The R function call pnorm(x) calculates that integral from -\infty to x. All we need to do is subtract.

Math Version

\small{ \begin{aligned} \text{coverage} &=\int_{-1.96-\frac{\text{bias}}{\text{se}}}^{+1.96-\frac{\text{bias}}{\text{se}}} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{u^2}{2}} du \\ &= \int_{-\infty}^{+1.96-\frac{\text{bias}}{\text{se}}} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{u^2}{2}} du \ - \ \int_{-\infty}^{-1.96-\frac{\text{bias}}{\text{se}}} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{u^2}{2}} du \end{aligned} }

R Version

coverage = function(bias.over.se) {  pnorm(1.96 - bias.over.se) - pnorm(-1.96 - bias.over.se)  }

Examples

 
 
\frac{\text{bias}}{\text{se}} = \frac{1}{2} \quad \implies \quad \text{coverage} = 92\%

 
 
\frac{\text{bias}}{\text{se}} = 1 \quad \implies \quad \text{coverage} = 83\%

 
 
\frac{\text{bias}}{\text{se}} = 2 \quad \implies \quad \text{coverage} = 48\%

 
 
\frac{\text{bias}}{\text{se}} = 3 \quad \implies \quad \text{coverage} = 15\%

  • Suppose we’ve got bias, but only a little bit.

  • We’re doing a pilot study with sample size n=100.

  • And we’re sure that our estimator’s absolute bias is less than half its standard error.

  • Question 1. What’s the coverage of our 95% confidence intervals?

  • A. At least 92%.
  • Now suppose we’re doing a follow-up study with sample size n=1600.
  • And we’re using the same estimator. Bias is the same.
  • Question 2. What’s the coverage of our 95% confidence intervals?
  • Assume our estimator’s standard error is proportional to \sqrt{1/n}.
    • This is the relation we used for independent sample means last class.
    • That means it’ll be \sqrt{1/1600} \ / \ \sqrt{1/100}=\sqrt{1/16}=1/4 of what it was in the pilot study.
  • A. At least 48%. Here’s why. \text{If } \ \frac{\abs{\text{bias}}}{\text{pilot se}} \le 1/2 \ \text{ and } \ \text{follow-up se} = \frac{\text{pilot se}}{4}, \ \text { then } \ \frac{\abs{\text{bias}}}{\text{follow-up se}} = 4 \times \frac{\abs{\text{bias}}}{\text{pilot se}} \le 2.

Back to Power

  • Remember: two-sided power to rule out a line is one minus the fraction of intervals that cover that line.
  • We already know how to calculate it. Replace bias in SE units by the distance from that line to the estimator’s center.

\text{two-sided power}=1-\text{coverage}(d),\qquad d=\frac{\text{estimator center}-\text{reference line}}{\text{se}}.

  • For success or futility, count only the tail on the side that answers the decision.
  • It is still the same normal-area calculation.

What If They Were Never Exactly Equal?

  • Outside physics and randomized experiments, two things are never exactly equal.
  • Testing whether they are only tells you whether you have enough power to say they aren’t.
