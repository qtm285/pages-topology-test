Warm-up: Computing Moments

Exercise 17.1 (One Deck, Two Sampling Mechanisms)  

Exercise

Draw three cards from a standard deck. Do every calculation twice: first replace and reshuffle each card before the next draw; then deal three cards without replacement. Let I_i indicate that card i is a heart, and let H=I_1+I_2+I_3.

  1. Calculate \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[H] in both designs. Explain why the answer is the same even though only one design has independent indicators.
  2. Calculate \mathop{\mathrm{\mathop{\mathrm{V}}}}(H) with replacement by centering and squaring out the sum.
  3. Without replacement, calculate \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[I_1I_2] and compare it with \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[I_1]\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[I_2]. Use the difference to calculate \mathop{\mathrm{Cov}}(I_1,I_2).
  4. Use the three variances and six ordered cross-terms to calculate \mathop{\mathrm{\mathop{\mathrm{V}}}}(H) without replacement. Which design is more precise, and where does the difference enter?
  5. Recover \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[H^2] in both designs from your means and variances.
  6. In each design, use Markov and Chebyshev to bound the probability of drawing three hearts. Then calculate the exact probability. What information does the exact calculation use that the two moment bounds discard?
SolutionSolution

Linearity does not require independence, so both designs have \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[H]=\sum_{i=1}^3\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[I_i]=3\left(\frac{13}{52}\right)=\frac34. With replacement, the indicators are independent and each has variance 3/16, so \mathop{\mathrm{\mathop{\mathrm{V}}}}(H)=9/16. Without replacement, \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[I_1I_2]=\frac{13}{52}\frac{12}{51}=\frac1{17}, \qquad \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[I_1]\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[I_2]=\frac1{16}, \qquad \mathop{\mathrm{Cov}}(I_1,I_2)=-\frac1{272}. Consequently, \mathop{\mathrm{\mathop{\mathrm{V}}}}(H)=3\left(\frac3{16}\right)+6\left(-\frac1{272}\right) =\frac{147}{272}. The negative covariances make dealing without replacement more precise. Finally, \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[H^2]=9/8 with replacement and 75/68 without replacement. Markov gives P(H=3)\le1/4. Since H=3 is 9/4 above its mean, Chebyshev gives 1/9 with replacement and 49/459 without replacement. The exact probabilities are (1/4)^3 with replacement and \binom{13}{3}/\binom{52}{3} without replacement.

Exercise 18.1 (What Is Independent?)  

Exercise

For each pair, say whether the two random variables are independent and identify the random mechanism that makes your answer true.

  1. The numbers shown on two separately rolled dice.
  2. Two successive cards dealt from one shuffled deck.
  3. Two separate fair coin flips.
  4. Two odds-and-evens players who each flip a separate independent fair coin.
  5. Two odds-and-evens players when the second player copies the first.
  6. The sum of two dice and the number on the first die.
SolutionSolution
  1. The two die results are independent when the dice are rolled separately.
  2. Successive cards from one shuffled deck are not independent: after the first card is known, the probabilities for the second card change.
  3. The two separate fair coin flips are independent.
  4. The players’ choices are independent because they use separate independent coins.
  5. The second player’s choice equals the first player’s choice, so the choices are dependent even though each player’s marginal distribution can look fair.
  6. The sum of the two dice and the first die are not independent. For example, if the sum is two, then the first die must be one.

Same Move, Many Surfaces

Exercise 19.1 (Variance of a Sum, the Conventional Way)  

Exercise

Let Y_1 \ldots Y_n be independent random variables with means \mu_1 \ldots \mu_n and finite variances.

  1. Center \sum_iY_i, square out the centered sum, and take expectations term by term.
  2. Explain exactly where independence is used and why every off-diagonal term is zero.
  3. Conclude that \mathop{\mathrm{\mathop{\mathrm{V}}}}(\sum_iY_i)=\sum_i\mathop{\mathrm{\mathop{\mathrm{V}}}}(Y_i).
  4. Compare this proof with the independent-copy proof from class. Which step is genuinely different, and which algebra merely appears in a different place?
  5. If A,B,C are independent, use the operational rule for independence to explain why A+B and C are independent.
SolutionSolution

Writing Z_i=Y_i-\mu_i, \begin{aligned} \mathop{\mathrm{\mathop{\mathrm{V}}}}\left(\sum_iY_i\right) &=\mathop{\mathrm{E}}\left[\left(\sum_iZ_i\right)^2\right] =\sum_i\sum_j\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Z_iZ_j]\\ &=\sum_i\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Z_i^2] =\sum_i\mathop{\mathrm{\mathop{\mathrm{V}}}}(Y_i). \end{aligned} For i\ne j, independence implies \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Z_iZ_j]=\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Z_i]\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Z_j]=0. The independent-copy proof avoids centering by the mean, but it still squares out a sum and kills mixed terms. Finally, independence of A,B,C means that their joint distribution factors. Summing that factorization over all values of A and B with the same value of A+B shows that the joint distribution of (A+B,C) factors. Therefore, for any functions f,g, \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[f(A+B)g(C)]=\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[f(A+B)]\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[g(C)].

Exercise 20.1 (Count Without Finding the Distribution)  

Exercise

Deal five cards from a shuffled standard deck without replacement.

  1. Let H be the number of hearts in the hand. Write H as a sum of five indicators.
  2. Use linearity to calculate \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[H]. Why does the dependence among the five indicators not matter for this calculation?
  3. Repeat the calculation for the number of aces.
  4. Generalize both answers to a hand of k cards.
  5. Which corresponding variance calculation would require information that linearity alone does not provide?
SolutionSolution

For each card position i, let I_i indicate that the card is a heart. Then the number of hearts is H=\sum_{i=1}^5 I_i. Each position contains a heart with probability 13/52=1/4, so linearity of expectation implies \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[H]=\sum_{i=1}^5\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[I_i]=5/4. The indicators are dependent, but linearity does not require independence. The same argument gives an expected ace count of 5(4/52)=5/13. For a hand of k cards, the expected counts are k/4 hearts and k/13 aces. Calculating either variance requires pairwise joint probabilities, because the squared sum contains products I_iI_j.

Exercise 21.1 (Tell a Story About a Joint Distribution)  

Exercise

The random pair (A,B) takes values in \{1,2\}\times\{1,2\}. Its joint probabilities are P(A=1,B=1)=P(A=2,B=2)=\frac12, \qquad P(A=1,B=2)=P(A=2,B=1)=0.

  1. Tell a concrete story whose random pair has this joint distribution.
  2. Calculate the marginal distributions of A and B.
  3. Someone sees only the two margins and says both variables look fair. What did they miss?
  4. Give a second joint distribution with the same margins in which A and B are independent.
SolutionSolution

Flip one fair coin. If it is heads, set (A,B)=(1,1); if it is tails, set (A,B)=(2,2). Then each variable is uniform on \{1,2\}: P(A=1)=P(A=2)=P(B=1)=P(B=2)=1/2. The margins hide the dependence: observing A determines B. An independent joint distribution with the same margins assigns probability 1/4 to each of the four pairs in \{1,2\}\times\{1,2\}.

Bias and Consistency

Using Prior Information

So far, we’ve exclusively talked about unbiased estimators. That is, estimators with the property that their expected value is equal to the estimation target. \hat\theta \qqtext{ is called unbiased if } \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[\hat{\theta}] = \theta. We say an estimator is biased if this isn’t true. To get a sense of what that means, let’s consider a simple example of a biased estimator. \tilde{Y}_1 = \frac{1}{n+1}\cdot \{\frac{1}{2} + \sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_i\}

Exercise 22.1  

Exercise

Calculate the expected value of the estimator \hat\theta =\tilde{Y}_1. Then calculate its bias \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[\hat\theta] - \theta and its standard deviation \sqrt{\mathop{\mathrm{\mathop{\mathrm{V}}}}[\hat\theta]}. Report all three.

SolutionSolution

\begin{aligned} \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[\tilde Y_1] &= \mathop{\mathrm{E}}\sb*{\frac{\frac{1}{2} + \sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_i}{n+1}} = \frac{\frac{1}{2} + \sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Y_i]}{n+1} = \frac{\frac{1}{2} + n\theta}{n+1} \\ \text{bias} &= \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[\tilde Y_1] - \theta = \frac{\frac{1}{2} + n\theta}{n+1} - \theta = \frac{\frac{1}{2} + n\theta - (n+1)\theta}{n+1} = \frac{\frac{1}{2} - \theta}{n+1} \\ \mathop{\mathrm{\mathop{\mathrm{V}}}}[\tilde Y_1] &= \mathop{\mathrm{E}}\sb*{ \left\{ * \right\}{\frac{\p*{\frac{1}{2} + \sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_i}}{n+1} - \frac{\frac{1}{2} + n\theta}{n+1}}^2 } \\ &= \mathop{\mathrm{E}}\sb*{ \left\{ * \right\}{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_i - \theta)}{n+1}}^2 } \\ &= \mathop{\mathrm{E}}\sb*{ \left\{ * \right\}{\frac{n}{n+1} \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_i - \theta)}^2 } \\ &= \p*{\frac{n}{n+1}}^2 \mathop{\mathrm{E}}\sb*{ \left\{ * \right\}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_i - \theta)}^2 } \\ &= \p*{\frac{n}{n+1}}^2 \mathop{\mathrm{\mathop{\mathrm{V}}}}[\bar Y] = \p*{\frac{n}{n+1}}^2 \frac{\theta(1-\theta)}{n} = \frac{n\theta(1-\theta)}{(n+1)^2} \qqtext{ so } \\ \mathop{\mathrm{sd}}(\tilde Y_1) &= \sqrt{\frac{n\theta(1-\theta)}{(n+1)^2}} \end{aligned}

This is a specific example of a more general estimator that integrates information from a prior study. Maybe a real study or maybe a study we’re imagining. Suppose we have \nprior observations \yprior_1 \ldots \yprior_{\nprior} from this study1 \thetaprior = \frac{1}{\nprior}\sum_{i=1}^{\nprior}\yprior_i.

Then averaging all our observations — from our current study and this prior one — gives us the following estimator.

\begin{aligned} \tilde{Y}_{\nprior} &= \frac{1}{\nprior + n} \left\{ * \right\}{\sum_{i=1}^{\nprior}\yprior_i + \sum_{i=1}^{n} Y_i} \\ &= \frac{1}{\nprior + n} \left\{ * \right\}{ \nprior\thetaprior + n\bar Y } \end{aligned}

We treat these prior observations, and therefore their mean \thetaprior, as deterministic. The simple example we started with, \tilde{Y}_1, is a special case where we have a single prior observation \yprior_1 = \frac{1}{2}.

Convergence in Probability

Now let’s think about another notion of convergence. If an estimator’s sampling distribution ends up in the right location (\text{bias} \to 0) with arbitrarily little spread (\text{variance} \to 0), then it makes sense that any draw from it will be close to the estimation target \mu.

Recall that you can visualize your estimator as a single dot drawn from its sampling distribution. Like the black one below. And if you want to think of what happens if you were to repeat your survey 100 times, you can visualize the results by plotting 100 dots. Like the purple ones.

We formalize this idea by talking about convergence in probability. We say an estimator \hat\mu converges in probability to \mu if, for anyone’s idea of `sufficiently close’, the probability that \hat\mu is sufficiently close to \mu goes to one. Or equivalently, the probability that it isn’t sufficiently close goes to zero. P(\lvert\hat\mu - \mu\rvert \ge \epsilon) \to 0 \qqtext{ as } n \to \infty

Convergence in mean square implies convergence in probability. Let’s see why. We’ll use Markov’s inequality.

Markov’s Inequality

Markov’s inequality says that the probability a nonnegative random variable X exceeds some threshold t is bounded by the ratio of its expected value and t, i.e., P(X \geq t) \leq \frac{\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[X]}{t} Often, instead of using this to bound the random variable we’re interested in directly, e.g. X=\lvert\hat\mu - \mu\rvert, we use it to bound its square. X \ge t if and only if X^2 \ge t^2, so the probability that X \ge t is the same as the probability that X^2 \ge t^2. Applying Markov’s inequality to the random variable X^2 gives us a bound in terms of X’s mean square. P(X \ge t) = P(X^2 \ge t^2) \leq \frac{\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[X^2]}{t^2} \qqtext{ e.g.} P(\lvert\hat\mu - \mu\rvert \ge \epsilon) = P((\hat\mu - \mu)^2 \ge \epsilon^2) \leq \frac{\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[(\hat\mu - \mu)^2]}{\epsilon^2}

This tells us, in terms of the mean squared error of \hat\mu, how likely it is that \hat\mu is far from \mu. And, in particular, it tells us that if the mean squared error goes to zero, then the probability that \hat\mu is any distance \epsilon away from \mu goes to zero.

Now we’re going to do two things. First, we’re going to prove Markov’s inequality. It’s good practice working with expectations and probabilities. Second, we’re going to look at what Markov’s inequality tells us about how close \hat\mu is to \mu, i.e., we’re going to use it to calculate the coverage of an interval estimate.

First we define the following two functions, f and g, with t, x\ge 0. f(x,t) = \frac{x}{t} g(x,t) = 1_{\ge t}(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{ if } x \ge t \\ 0 & \text{ otherwise } \end{cases}

Markov’s Inequality and Interval Estimation

So far, when we’ve calculated the coverage of an interval estimate in terms of its mean and variance, we’ve relied on normal approximation. That is, we’ve been using a different formula for P(\lvert\hat\mu - \mu\rvert \ge \epsilon) based on the idea that \hat\mu-\mu has approximately the same distribution as a normally-distributed random variable Z with the same mean—zero—and variance \sigma_{\hat\mu}^2=\mathop{\mathrm{\mathop{\mathrm{V}}}}[\hat\mu-\mu]. We can use this approximation, in combination with our knowledge of the probability density of the normal approximation, to get a bound on the probability that \lvert\hat\mu - \mu\rvert \ge \epsilon.

P(\abs{\hat\mu - \mu} \ge \epsilon) \approx P(\abs{Z} \ge \epsilon) = 1-P(\abs{Z} < \epsilon) = 1- \int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon} f_{\sigma_{\hat\mu}}(z) dz \qfor f_{\sigma}(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma} e^{-z^2/(2\sigma^2)}

The reason we’re always talking about intervals of the form \hat\mu \pm 2 \sigma_{\hat\mu} is that, using this approximation, that yields 95% coverage. That is, P(\abs{Z} < 2 \sigma_{\hat\mu}) \approx .95. To see why we tend to use normal approximation instead of Markov’s inequality, let’s see what Markov’s inequality tells us about how close an estimator is to its estimation target and about the coverage of this interval.

I didn’t ask for this here, but let me offer an interpretation. Where normality of our estimator’s sampling distribution would imply 95% coverage for the interval \hat\mu \pm 2\sigma_{\hat\mu}, Markov’s inequality implies 75%. That’s why we don’t use Markov’s inequality for much if we have a better alternative, e.g., if normal approximation is justified or we can use the bootstrap. It’s not wrong, but what we can say based on it is more conservative than what we can say based on other techniques.

Comparing Interval Calibration Methods

Let X equal one with probability p and zero with probability 1-p.

  1. List the two possible values of |X-p|^3 and their probabilities.
  2. Calculate \mathop{\mathrm{E}}|X-p|^3.
  3. For n independent copies, calculate the sum of the n individual third absolute centered moments.
  4. The later normal-approximation result uses this sum. Why is that easier than calculating the distribution—or the third absolute centered moment—of the sum itself?
SolutionSolution

The two values of |X-p|^3 are (1-p)^3 with probability p and p^3 with probability 1-p. Therefore \mathop{\mathrm{E}}|X-p|^3=p(1-p)^3+(1-p)p^3. For n independent copies, the sum of the individual third absolute centered moments is n times this quantity. No distribution of a sum has been calculated.

Paley–Zygmund: The Other Half

Exercise 25.1 (Bonus: A Lower Bound on Spread)  

Exercise

For a nonnegative random variable Z and 0<a<1, the Paley–Zygmund inequality says P(Z\ge a\mathop{\mathrm{E}}Z)\ge (1-a)^2\frac{(\mathop{\mathrm{E}}Z)^2}{\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Z^2]}. Let Y have mean \mu, positive variance \sigma^2, and finite fourth centered moment. Apply the inequality with Z=(Y-\mu)^2 and a=1/2.

  1. Rewrite the event Z\ge a\mathop{\mathrm{E}}Z as a statement about |Y-\mu| and \sigma.
  2. Express the resulting lower bound using \sigma^4 and \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[(Y-\mu)^4].
  3. Chebyshev says there cannot be too much probability far from \mu. Explain how this result says that the standard deviation cannot be describing spread that is not really there.
SolutionSolution

Since \mathop{\mathrm{E}}Z=\sigma^2 and \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Z^2]=\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[(Y-\mu)^4], P\left(|Y-\mu|\ge\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt2}\right) \ge\frac{\sigma^4}{4\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[(Y-\mu)^4]}. Chebyshev supplies an upper bound on distant tails; this supplies a lower bound on a standard-deviation-scale departure when the fourth moment is controlled.
