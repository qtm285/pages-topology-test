To See What’s Easy, Revisit Something Hard

We’ve worked out the exact formulas for these distributions in the previous two chapters. The distributions we get when we sample with and without replacement, when we have binary responses. It was a lot of work. We went from call sequences to response sequences, then from response sequences to their sum. We needed a count of the binary sequences with each sum. And without replacement, we had to keep track of who each call used up.

These are called the Binomial and Hypergeometric distributions.

In the bootstrap homework, we also looked at the sampling distribution we got when we used a particular form of randomized convenience sampling, where we start at a random position on a circle. In “One Roll and a Walk”, we canvassed a street of ten houses on a walk that loops from house 10 back to house 1, rolled the d10 once, and knocked at the house it named and the next three. That sampling distribution is neither Binomial nor Hypergeometric.

All of this was a lot of work, and these were probably the simplest cases that are relevant to real practice. What we’re going to do now is talk about some principles that will let us get at these approximately, without working them out in full. In particular, we will talk about location and spread.

To see what’s easy, we’re going to revisit something hard: how we calculated these distributions in the first place. What stays the same when we change the sampling scheme or the population?

How We Got Here

Everything so far has been about binary outcomes. Each person in the population would say Yes or No, which we write as 1 or 0, and the total is the number of Yeses. To get these distributions, we listed the call sequences, replaced each person called by their response, and added up the probabilities of the sequences with the same total.

Two Calls

Every call sequence is equally likely. That follows from the design; it is not assumed. With replacement, each call is an independent uniform pick, so all sixteen ordered pairs of people are equally likely. Without replacement, the sample is a shuffle, so all twelve ordered pairs of distinct people are.

Margins and Independence

Now we look at one or two calls at a time: what the distribution of a single call is, and when single calls tell us about pairs.

One or Two Calls out of Three

Suppose we only want the distribution of one call, or of a pair. Everything is set up, so let’s do it mechanically first.

To get from three calls to two, we add across the layers. If we fix a first call and a second call, the four cells in that position, one in each layer, are the four possibilities for the third call, and adding them gives 4/64 = 1/16 — the two-call grid back, cell for cell. And nothing picked out the first two calls: if we fix any pair of the three and add over the remaining one, the same thing happens.

Now, why didn’t you have to do that? The first call, obviously: everyone is equally likely to be picked. But what’s special about the first? Nothing. That’s the argument.

The principle behind each of these sampling schemes is uniformity among all possible call sequences. The possible set varies — every sequence of n people with replacement, every sequence of n distinct people without — but on that set, all sequences are equally likely. If all sequences are equally likely, then the subsequences sitting in any pair of slots are equally likely too. So is the one sitting in any single slot. Otherwise the whole premise was false.

If you’re rolling a die, it doesn’t matter how many times you roll it. On each individual roll, or each pair of rolls, the same thing happens. It’s maybe a little less obvious when you shuffle a deck. But if you shuffle a deck and draw the top card, or shuffle a deck and then cut it and draw the top card, or the top two, the cut isn’t going to change anything. All configurations are equally likely after the cut. And that’s true if you cut twice: the slots you take don’t have to be consecutive. If you could change the distribution of a deck by cutting it, you wouldn’t be allowed to cut it.

So the calculation was a painstaking way of seeing that. The calculation is useful to get the distribution of the sum. But to get down to a smaller sample size, we didn’t really have to do it. It just falls off the principle of the thing. From sequences to subsequences.

What Stays the Same

To get from two calls to one, we sum a row or a column. That gives the distribution of one call, which is called its marginal distribution, or its margin for short.

The first row is every call sequence whose first call is person 1, and it adds to 1/4. The first column is every sequence whose second call is person 1, and it adds to 1/4 too. The same holds for each of the other three people, and grouping the people by their response turns that into the distribution of one response.

Without replacement, each row and each column has three possible cells of 1/12, so each person still has probability 1/4 on either call. Changing the population changes the responses attached to those people, not their chance of being called. With two voters the chance of hearing Yes was 1/2; with one voter it is 1/4. For either fixed population, that response probability is the same under both schemes.

The Rules, on the Grid

A random variable’s distribution assigns a number P(X=x) to each value x it can take. Two properties define it. Every one of those numbers is at least zero, and they sum to one over all the values. P(X=x)\ge0 \qqtext{ for every } x, \qquad \sum_x P(X=x) = 1. Everything else about probabilities follows from these two and one more rule. If two things cannot both happen, the probability that one of them does is the sum of their probabilities. That’s called disjoint additivity. For a set S of values, because X cannot take two values at once, P(X \in S) = \sum_{x \in S} P(X=x).

Independence

Here are the two response grids for the population with one voter.

With replacement.

Without replacement.

One call on its own looks the same with or without replacement, but two calls together don’t: with replacement, every cell of the two-call grid is the product of the one-call probabilities, and without replacement that is not true.

In both of them each response is a Yes with probability 1/4. For the first response it is the sum of the bottom row of either grid, and for the second it is the sum of the right column. The margins are identical. Now we look inside.

With replacement, the probability that both are Yes is 1/16, which is (1/4)\times(1/4) — the product of the two margins. So is another cell: 3/16 is (3/4)\times(1/4). And 9/16 is (3/4)\times(3/4). Every cell is the product of its two margins.

Without replacement, the probability that both are Yes is zero, where the product still says 1/16. There is only one voter to reach, so hearing Yes twice is impossible — and the margins never knew that.

The grids above show what’s called the joint distribution of J_1 and J_2, and of Y_1 and Y_2.

When every cell of the joint distribution is the product of its two margins, the two random variables are called independent. When some cell isn’t, they’re called dependent. Sampling with replacement gives us independent calls; sampling without replacement does not.

That tells us something about the margins. Knowing the distribution of each call separately does not tell us which of these grids we are in. For a fixed population, the margins are what both designs share. The joint tells sampling with replacement from sampling without it, so the joint is where we have to look when a question is about two calls at once.

Four live cells out of sixteen, a quarter each, because there are only four places to start. Adding across any row or down any column gives 1/4.

We aggregate twice again.

For this population, the response grid and total distribution are the same as under sampling without replacement. The call grids differ: the cycle permits only four of the twelve distinct-person pairs. Both designs nevertheless reach the single voter on either call with probability 1/4.

Random Variables

Now we turn to location and spread. Both are summaries of a random variable’s distribution, so we start with what a random variable is.

Observations as Random Variables

The notation y_j refers to a number. It’s the response of the jth person in our population. That’s just some person. The notation Y_i refers to something else. It’s the response of the ith person we call in our survey. That’s not a person. That’s the result of a random process—the roll of a die. To summarize this result, we talk about the probability distribution of Y_i.

NoteNotation Conventions
  • Random variables are written in uppercase: X, Y, Z, etc. Constants are written in lowercase: x, y, z, etc.
  • We’ll use the same letter for a random variable and the value it takes on. x is a possible value of X, y of Y, etc.
  • Estimators are also random variables, but instead of uppercase we use a hat: \hat \mu, \hat \sigma, etc.
  • We’ll use the same letter for the estimator and what it’s meant to estimate. \hat \mu is an estimator of \mu.

Expectation

Here’s the definition. The expectation of a random variable is its probability-weighted average, \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[X] = \sum_x \textcolor{#EF476F}{x}\textcolor{#118AB2}{P(X=x)}. That means summing, over the x axis, the product of the x, the position along the x-axis and the P(X=x), the bar height at that position.

Remember: histograms are for counting dots. The expectation is the average of infinitely many dots.

Linearity

Getting a sampling distribution meant building the joint: a grid for two calls, a cube for three, a layer more for every call after that. What’s so special about expectations that they don’t make us do that? Linearity.

Here is the response grid for our population again, three zeros and one one, sampling with replacement.

Rows are the first response and columns the second; each cell contains the probability of that response pair.

The long way to the total S=Y_1+Y_2 reads the cells off the grid: each cell’s total times that cell’s probability, added up. The top left cell is two zeros, so its total is 0; the two off-diagonal cells have total 1; the bottom right cell is two ones, so its total is 2, \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[S]=0\times\colorbox{#eadff1}{$\displaystyle \frac9{16}$}+1\times\colorbox{#dbeef5}{$\displaystyle \frac3{16}$}+1\times\colorbox{#f9e0e7}{$\displaystyle \frac3{16}$}+2\times\colorbox{#dff0df}{$\displaystyle \frac1{16}$} =\frac{8}{16}=\frac12.

That same sum regroups into a first-call pass and a second-call pass. Every cell’s total is the first response plus the second, so \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[S]=\underbrace{0\times\colorbox{#FCE4EC}{$\displaystyle \frac{9+3}{16}$}+1\times\colorbox{#FCE4EC}{$\displaystyle \frac{3+1}{16}$}}_{\text{the rows}} \;+\;\underbrace{0\times\colorbox{#E0F2F7}{$\displaystyle \frac{9+3}{16}$}+1\times\colorbox{#E0F2F7}{$\displaystyle \frac{3+1}{16}$}}_{\text{the columns}} =\frac14+\frac14=\frac12. The two passes come out the same here because both calls have the same distribution; what matters is what each used. The first pass used only the row totals, and the rows are the distribution of the first response. The second used only the column totals, which are the distribution of the second. So \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Y_1+Y_2]=\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Y_1]+\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Y_2]. The joint entered only through its margins.

We didn’t need the whole grid, only its margins. And we didn’t need the grid to get the margins: they are the one-call distributions, which we already had.

The same regrouping works for three calls, or n, without the cube.

\begin{aligned} \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[aY+bZ] &=\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[aY]+\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[bZ]\\ &=a\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Y]+b\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Z] \qqtext{for random variables} Y, Z \qqtext{and numbers} a, b. \end{aligned}

There are two things going on here. To average a sum of two things, we can take two averages and sum. To average a constant times a random variable, we multiply the random variable’s average by the constant. In other words, we can distribute expectations and can pull constants out of them. These rules do not require the two random variables to be independent.

Proof of Linearity

In essence, it comes down to the fact that all we’re doing is summing. Expectations are probability-weighted sums. And we’re looking at the expectation of a sum. And we can change the order we sum in without changing what we get. The marginalization step sums along a row or column of the joint distribution.

\small{ \begin{aligned} \mathop{\mathrm{E}}\p*{ a Y + b Z } &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{ by definition of expectation}}{=} \sum_{y}\sum_z (a y + b z) \ P(Y=y, Z=z) \\ &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{changing the order in which we sum}}{=} \sum_{y}\sum_z a y \ P(Y=y, Z=z) + \sum_{z}\sum_y b z \ P(Y=y, Z=z) \\ &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{pulling constants out of the inner sums}}{=} \sum_{y} a y \ \sum_z P(Y=y,Z=z) + \sum_{z} b z \ \sum_y P(Y=y,Z=z) \\ &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{summing to get marginal probabilities from our joint }}{=} \sum_{y} a y \ P(Y=y) + \sum_{z} b z \ P(Z=z) \\ &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{ pulling constants out of the remaining sum }}{=} a\sum_{y} y \ P(Y=y) + b\sum_{z} z \ P(Z=z) \\ &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{by definition}}{=} a\mathop{\mathrm{E}}Y + b \mathop{\mathrm{E}}Z. \end{aligned} }

Probability as Expectation

A tool we use here is understanding probability as an expectation. We write the indicator that call i selected person j, 1_{=j}(J_i) = \begin{cases} 1 & \qqtext{ for } J_i = j \\ 0 & \qqtext{ otherwise} \end{cases} It is one on exactly the calls that landed on person j, so its probability-weighted average is the probability of landing there, \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[1_{=j}(J_i)] = 0\,P(J_i\ne j) + 1\,P(J_i=j) = P(J_i=j).

This is a principle, not a notational trick: probabilities are expectations of indicators. The indicator keeps exactly the part of the random outcome that matters for the event and throws everything else away. Its probability-weighted average is the probability that it is one. Consequently, every rule we prove for expectations is also a tool for probabilities.

For a picture of the same operation on a different event, let X count the Yeses in five independent calls to a population where 40% say Yes.

On the right is the distribution of 1_{\ge3}(X), which has just two outcomes: it is zero with probability P(X<3) and one with probability P(X\ge3)=0.317. That height is both P(X\ge3) and \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[1_{\ge3}(X)] — the same number read two ways.

Independence as a Property of Expectations

The factorization from the two-call grid works for any functions f and g of independent finite-valued random variables X and Y: \begin{aligned} \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[f(X)g(Y)] &=\sum_x\sum_y f(x)g(y)P(X=x,Y=y)\\ &=\sum_x\sum_y f(x)g(y)P(X=x)P(Y=y)\\ &=\left\{\sum_x f(x)P(X=x)\right\} \left\{\sum_y g(y)P(Y=y)\right\}\\ &=\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[f(X)]\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[g(Y)]. \end{aligned} The reverse implication holds as well: if this property holds for every pair of functions f and g, then X and Y are independent. So the two are equivalent, and some people take the expectation property as the definition. To see why the reverse holds, let f and g be indicators, f=1_{=x} and g=1_{=y}. Their product 1_{=x}(X)1_{=y}(Y) is one when X=x and Y=y and zero otherwise, so \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[1_{=x}(X)1_{=y}(Y)]=P(X=x,Y=y) \qqtext{and} \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[1_{=x}(X)]\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[1_{=y}(Y)]=P(X=x)P(Y=y). If the expectation property holds for these f and g, every joint probability is the product of the two marginal probabilities. That is independence.

Variance

The expectation gives the center, and the average squared distance from it gives the spread.

Variance Is an Expectation

The centered variable Z=Y-\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Y] is a random variable with its mean subtracted; it records a signed distance from the mean. Its expectation is zero: \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Z]=\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Y]-\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Y]=0. Positive and negative distances cancel. To measure their size, square them before averaging. The variance is the expected squared distance from the mean: \operatorname{Var}(Y)=\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[(Y-\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Y])^2]=\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Z^2]. The picture follows twenty centered observations.

Data. Each dot is one centered observation. The histogram counts them. Positive and negative distances cancel in their average, zero.

Barbells. We keep every data dot and connect it to its signed square: for example, -1.5 moves to -2.25, while 0.5 moves to 0.25. The sign still records which side of the mean the observation came from.

Signed-square distribution. We replace the histogram by the distribution of the signed-square endpoints. The original dots and connecting barbells remain, so we can follow each observation through the transformation.

Fold and average. We fold the negative histogram onto the positive side and stack their probabilities. The data dots and barbells remain as a reference. The new mean line marks the average squared distance—the variance.

The dots are their signed distances from the mean, so the mean line starts at zero. A bar’s height records the fraction of observations at that distance. To square a distance, first square its length on its original side of zero, then fold the negative side over. The original dots and the barbells connecting them to their signed squares stay visible as the distribution changes. Observations from the negative side keep one color and those from the positive side the other. The final histogram counts squared distances, whose average is the variance.

Squaring changes the units. The standard deviation takes us back to the units of Y: \operatorname{sd}(Y)=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(Y)}.

Exercise 11.2  

Exercise

What happens to variance and standard deviation when we add the same number to every value? What happens when we multiply every value by two? By minus two?

SolutionSolution

Adding a constant b moves the mean by the same amount, leaving all distances unchanged. Multiplying by a constant a multiplies signed distances by a, squared distances by a^2, and standard deviation by |a|: \operatorname{Var}(aY+b)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(Y), \qquad \operatorname{sd}(aY+b)=|a|\operatorname{sd}(Y).
