Sampling, Location, and Spread

The population has 100 people: 40 respond one and 60 respond zero. Compare samples of 5, 20, and 80 people under three mechanisms:

  • With replacement: select each person uniformly and independently from the whole population.
  • Without replacement: select uniformly from the people not yet chosen.
  • Random-start cycle: arrange the 40 ones followed by the 60 zeros around a circle. Select a starting person uniformly, then take consecutive people.

Where are the distributions of the sample average located? How do their spreads compare? What changes as the sample grows?

Code 
m.frame = 100
theta.frame = .4
population.frame = c(rep(1, 40), rep(0, 60))
n.frame = c(5, 20, 80)

sampling.distribution = function(n, mechanism) {
  if(mechanism == "With replacement") {
    counts = 0:n
    probability = dbinom(counts, n, theta.frame)
    estimate = counts/n
  } else if(mechanism == "Without replacement") {
    counts = 0:n
    probability = dhyper(counts, 40, 60, n)
    estimate = counts/n
  } else {
    estimate = sapply(1:m.frame, function(start) {
      positions = ((start - 1 + 0:(n - 1)) %% m.frame) + 1
      mean(population.frame[positions])
    })
    tab = table(estimate)
    estimate = as.numeric(names(tab))
    probability = as.numeric(tab)/m.frame
  }
  data.frame(n, mechanism, estimate, probability)
}

design.distributions = purrr::map_dfr(
  n.frame,
  function(n) purrr::map_dfr(
    c("With replacement", "Without replacement", "Random-start cycle"),
    function(mechanism) sampling.distribution(n, mechanism)
  )
)

design.dots = design.distributions |>
  mutate(dot.count=round(400*probability)) |>
  tidyr::uncount(dot.count) |>
  group_by(n, mechanism, estimate) |>
  mutate(dot.height=row_number()/400) |>
  ungroup()

Random Variables

Naming Objects We Have Already Used

Each highlighted dot in the opening picture is one sample’s average. The green line marks the fixed population mean.

We have already been using objects such as Y_i, \sum_iY_i, and \hat\theta=n^{-1}\sum_iY_i. We will now call them random variables. The name is new; the objects are not. A random variable is a handle on a probability distribution. It lets us write a draw, a count, or an average and do arithmetic with it without first expanding its whole distribution.

NoteNotation Conventions
  • Random variables are written in uppercase: X, Y, Z, etc. Constants are written in lowercase: x, y, z, etc.
  • We’ll use the same letter for a random variable and the value it takes on. x is a possible value of X, y of Y, etc.
  • Estimators are also random variables, but instead of uppercase we use a hat: \hat \mu, \hat \sigma, etc.
  • We’ll use the same letter for the estimator and what it’s meant to estimate. \hat \mu is an estimator of \mu.

Defining Expectation

  • The expectation of a random variable is its probability-weighted average.

\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[X] = \sum_x \textcolor{#EF476F}{x}\textcolor{#118AB2}{P(X=x)}

  • That means summing, over the x axis, the product of …
    • … the x, the position along the x-axis
    • … the P(X=x), the bar height at that position.
  • Does that tell us where distribution is located, i.e., where draws are likely to be?
  • If not, why is that useful?

For a function of the same random variable, we average its values with the same probabilities, \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[f(Y)]=\sum_y f(y)P(Y=y). If Y is binary, this becomes \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Y]=0P(Y=0)+1P(Y=1)=P(Y=1).

Quick Practice

  • What is the expectation of …
    1. X, a binary random variable that’s one with probability \theta
    2. X+X=2X
    3. 2X-1
    4. X + Y where Y has the same distribution as X
    5. X^2
    6. XY

Interpretation

  • Remember: histograms are for counting dots.
  • The expectation is the average of infinitely many dots.
    • That is called the law of large numbers; next time.

What do we do when we don’t know the formula for the sampling distribution

  • Operationally. Bootstrap it. We can get valid confidence intervals without any calculation.
  • Theoretically. Calculate its first and second moments to characterize its location and spread.

Probability as Expectation

A tool we use here is understanding probability as an expectation. P(Z \in A) = \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[1_A(Z)] \qfor 1_A(z) = \begin{cases} 1 & \qqtext{ for } z \in A \\ 0 & \qqtext{ otherwise} \end{cases}

This is a principle, not a notational trick: probabilities are expectations of indicators. The indicator keeps exactly the part of the random outcome that matters for the event and throws everything else away. Its probability-weighted average is the probability that it is one. Consequently, every rule we prove for expectations is also a tool for probabilities.

Linearity of Expectations

\begin{aligned} E ( a Y + b Z ) &= E (aY) + E (bZ) \\ &= aE(Y) + bE(Z) \\ & \text{ for random variables $Y, Z$ and numbers $a,b$ } \end{aligned}

There are two things going on here. To average a sum of two things, we can take two averages and sum. To average a constant times a random variable, we multiply the random variable’s average by the constant. In other words, we can distribute expectations and can pull constants out of them. These rules do not require the two random variables to be independent.

NoteProof

In essence, it comes down to the fact that all we’re doing is summing. Expectations are probability-weighted sums. And we’re looking at the expectation of a sum. And we can change the order we sum in without changing what we get. The marginalization step sums along a row or column of the joint distribution.

\small{ \begin{aligned} \mathop{\mathrm{E}}\p*{ a Y + b Z } &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{ by definition of expectation}}{=} \sum_{y}\sum_z (a y + b z) \ P(Y=y, Z=z) \\ &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{changing the order in which we sum}}{=} \sum_{y}\sum_z a y \ P(Y=y, Z=z) + \sum_{z}\sum_y b z \ P(Y=y, Z=z) \\ &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{pulling constants out of the inner sums}}{=} \sum_{y} a y \ \sum_z P(Y=y,Z=z) + \sum_{z} b z \ \sum_y P(Y=y,Z=z) \\ &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{summing to get marginal probabilities from our joint }}{=} \sum_{y} a y \ P(Y=y) + \sum_{z} b z \ P(Z=z) \\ &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{ pulling constants out of the remaining sum }}{=} a\sum_{y} y \ P(Y=y) + b\sum_{z} z \ P(Z=z) \\ &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{by definition}}{=} a\mathop{\mathrm{E}}Y + b \mathop{\mathrm{E}}Z \end{aligned} }

Return to the expectation practice: which questions can you now answer using only the individual distributions? What information is still missing for the product?

Monotonicity of Expectations

If a(x)\le b(x) for every possible value x of X, then \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[a(X)]\le \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[b(X)]. The proof is just the definition of expectation. Every difference b(x)-a(x) is nonnegative, and every probability is nonnegative, so \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[b(X)]-\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[a(X)] =\sum_x \{b(x)-a(x)\}P(X=x)\ge 0. Next time we will use this result in a picture: draw a simple function above an indicator, then take expectations of both sides to turn the picture into a probability bound.

Sampling Uniformly at Random

A die roll: equally likely to be any side. The top of a shuffled deck: equally likely to be any card. Where a spinner lands: equally likely to be in any of its equal-sized sectors. These are ways to select uniformly at random.

For sampling, the possibilities are people. Number the people in a population from 1 to m. Sampling one person uniformly at random means each person has probability 1/m of being selected.

What happens when we select two people? Consider a population of four. Let J_1 be the first person selected and J_2 the second. Rows in these tables identify the first person; columns identify the second.

The joint distributions differ. Without replacement, we cannot select the same person twice. But sum across a row or down a column: the marginal distributions are the same. Each draw is uniformly distributed over the four people.

Even the second draw without replacement is uniform. Once we know the first person, only three possibilities remain. Before we know the first person, all four are equally likely to be second.

Average over the Population

Let the fixed population values be y_1\ldots y_m, with population mean \mu. On draw i we select person J_i and observe Y_i=y_{J_i}.

Use an indicator for each possible selection. Exactly one is one, so this sum picks out the selected person’s value:

Y_i=\sum_{j=1}^{m}y_j1_{\{J_i=j\}}.

Linearity and the indicator trick turn its expectation into the population average:

\begin{aligned} \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Y_i] &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{linearity}}{=}\sum_{j=1}^{m}y_j\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[1_{\{J_i=j\}}]\\ &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{probability as expectation}}{=}\sum_{j=1}^{m}y_jP(J_i=j)\\ &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{uniform sampling}}{=}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}y_j\\ &=\mu. \end{aligned}

The same calculation applies to any function of the response: \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[f(Y_i)]=\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^m f(y_j). This calculation does not use independence.

Unbiasedness of the Sample Mean

The sample mean is \hat\mu=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^nY_i. Claim. It is an unbiased estimator of the population mean. \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[\hat\mu] = \mu

\begin{aligned} \mathop{\mathrm{E}}\sb*{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i} &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{via linearity}}{=} \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Y_i] \\ &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{via equal-probability sampling}}{=} \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \mu \\ &= \frac1n \times n \times \mu = \mu. \end{aligned}

The sample mean is an unbiased estimator of the population mean. We have worked out its location without constructing its distribution. Next chapter we do the same for its spread.

Lack of independence does not make calculating the expectation of a mean any harder. Why not? Because linearity of expectation doesn’t require independence.

What Is Special about the Binomial?

Suppose two of our four people have response zero and two have response one. Under either sampling scheme, each response is equally likely to be zero or one. The sample average is unbiased under either scheme.

But the draws with replacement are independent. In the first table, learning which row we are in leaves the second person equally likely to be any of the four. In the second table, learning the row rules someone out.

The difference survives when we look at responses. With replacement, a first response of one leaves the probability of another one at 1/2. Without replacement, it falls to 1/3: only one of the remaining three people has response one.

The binomial counts ones in independent binary draws with the same success probability. Equal individual probabilities explain the expected count; independence is the additional property. We now need to understand what it lets us do with products, because calculating spread involves squaring a sum.

Independence

Definition. Random variables Y and Z are independent if \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[f(Y)g(Z)] = \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[f(Y)]\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[g(Z)] \qqtext{for all functions $f$ and $g$} Here we work with random variables taking finitely many values.

Equivalent. Their joint distribution is the product of their marginal distributions, P(Y=y,Z=z)=P(Y=y)P(Z=z). Knowing the value of one then does not change the probabilities for the other.

NoteWhy These Are Equivalent

Use the indicator functions f(Y)=1_{\{y\}}(Y) and g(Z)=1_{\{z\}}(Z) in the definition to obtain the joint-probability statement. In the other direction, \begin{aligned} \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[f(Y)g(Z)] &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{definition of expectation}}{=}\sum_y\sum_z f(y)g(z)P(Y=y,Z=z)\\ &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{factored joint probabilities}}{=}\sum_y\sum_z f(y)g(z)P(Y=y)P(Z=z)\\ &=\textcolor{#EF476F}{\left\{\sum_y f(y)P(Y=y)\right\}} \textcolor{#118AB2}{\left\{\sum_z g(z)P(Z=z)\right\}}\\ &=\textcolor{#EF476F}{\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[f(Y)]}\textcolor{#118AB2}{\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[g(Z)]}. \end{aligned}

Two Draws

Return to the four voters from class: two have response zero and two have response one. With replacement, the sixteen ordered pairs of calls are equally likely. Grouping pairs by their responses produces our partially marginalized table.

p Y_1 Y_2 Y_1 + Y_2
\color{#EF476F}\frac{4}{16} 0 0 0
\color{#118AB2}\frac{4}{16} 0 1 1
\color{#118AB2}\frac{4}{16} 1 0 1
\color{#06D6A0}\frac{4}{16} 1 1 2

To find the distribution of Y_1, sum over Y_2. To find the distribution of Y_2, sum over Y_1.

Value P(Y_1=\text{value}) P(Y_2=\text{value})
0 4/16+4/16=1/2 4/16+4/16=1/2
1 4/16+4/16=1/2 4/16+4/16=1/2

Each joint probability is 1/4=(1/2)(1/2), the product of its two marginal probabilities. That is independence in this table.

For a population with m_0 zeros and m_1 ones, where m_0+m_1=m, the same counting produces P(Y_1=a,Y_2=b)=\frac{m_am_b}{m^2} =\frac{m_a}{m}\frac{m_b}{m} =P(Y_1=a)P(Y_2=b),\qquad a,b\in\{0,1\}. Replacement leaves all m people available on the second call. More generally, independent rolls make each ordered pair of people have probability 1/m^2; grouping people by response preserves that product.

That’s what happens when the randomness in each Y_i comes from a different roll of the die. And since that’s how we’re doing our sampling in the Current Population Survey, it’ll be true in our sample. Because we draw each of our observations the same way, they also have the same probability distribution. We say they’re independent and identically distributed. At least, that’s what we’re pretending when we analyze CPS data in this class. Reality is more complicated.

NoteCollapsing the Calls into Responses

When our outcomes are binary, it’s easy to describe this distribution. All we need to know is the probability that Y_i is 1. To do that, we sum the probabilities of the rolls that result in it being one, P(Y_i = 1) = \sum_{j: y_j = 1} P(\text{roll}_i = j) = \sum_{j: y_j = 1} \frac{1}{m} \times y_j = \mu. This collapses out information about our random process that’s irrelevant to Y_i. That is, it collapses the probability we roll each number in 1…m into our ‘weighted coin flip’.

When our outcomes are nonbinary, it’s a bit more complicated. We need to know the probability that Y_i takes on each possible value. But we calculate it the same way—we sum the probabilities of the rolls that result in it taking on those values, P(Y_i = y) = \sum_{j: y_j = y} P(\text{roll}_i = j). We’re still collapsing out irrelevant information, but what we’re left with is more complicated. It’s a weighted die roll, with one face for each possible value of Y_i. And if each person’s income is different, then there’s nothing to collapse out, P(Y_i = y) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{m} & \qqtext{for} y \in y_1 \ldots y_m \\ 0 & \qqtext{otherwise} \end{cases}

Independence in a Game

Independence lives in the joint distribution, not in either variable by itself. Suppose two players in odds-and-evens each show either one or two fingers. Under fair play, both privately randomize, so the four possible pairs have probability 1/4. Now suppose the player who wins on an even sum secretly copies the other player’s choice. Only (1,1) and (2,2) occur, each with probability 1/2. Each player’s individual distribution still looks perfectly fair: each shows one or two half the time. The cheating appears only in the random pair (A,B). Knowing A now determines B.

Sampling without replacement is the same kind of warning in a familiar setting. Each draw has the right marginal distribution, but the first draw changes what can happen on the second.

Squaring Sums

\left\{ * \right\}{\sum_{i=1}^n Z_i}^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n Z_i Z_j

This is a generalization of the identity (a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2 to more terms. You may be so used to that you don’t even think about what’s really happening. Here’s a version where I’m very explicit: it’s a product of two copies of (a+b), one pink and one teal.

(a+b)^2 = \textcolor{#EF476F}{(a+b)}\textcolor{#118AB2}{(a+b)} = \textcolor{#EF476F}{a}\textcolor{#118AB2}{a} + \textcolor{#EF476F}{a}\textcolor{#118AB2}{b} + \textcolor{#EF476F}{b}\textcolor{#118AB2}{a} + \textcolor{#EF476F}{b}\textcolor{#118AB2}{b} = a^2 + 2ab + b^2

How do we generalize this to more terms? We’ll use the same color-coded-copies trick. It helps to count out the terms in our pink copy using i and in our teal copy using j.1 When we multiply out our sums, we get a product term for each pair of terms in the sum. Each product term involves one term in the pink sum and one term in the teal one.

\left\{ * \right\}{\sum_{i=1}^n Z_i}^2 = \textcolor{#EF476F}{\sum_{i=1}^n Z_i} \textcolor{#118AB2}{\sum_{j=1}^n Z_j} = \textcolor{#EF476F}{\sum_{i=1}^n} \textcolor{#118AB2}{\sum_{j=1}^n} \textcolor{#EF476F}{Z_i} \textcolor{#118AB2}{Z_j}

Cumulative Distribution Functions

One way to package the whole distribution is its cumulative distribution function, F(y)=P(Y\le y). This function answers every threshold question at once, and later it will let us compare a sampling distribution with a normal approximation. It is theoretically useful. It is also a terrible way to look at a random variable: every CDF starts near zero, climbs monotonically, and ends near one. Very different distributions become variations on the same rising shape. Histograms and probability-mass plots let us see the distribution’s shape; a CDF is a tool we reach for when its particular cumulative information is what we need.
