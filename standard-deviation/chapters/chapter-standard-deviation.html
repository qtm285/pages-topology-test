Variance

Variance is the average squared distance from the expectation. Define the centered response Z_i=Y_i-\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Y_i].

Its sign tells us which side of the center the response falls on. Its square measures squared distance from that center, \mathop{\mathrm{\mathop{\mathrm{V}}}}[Y_i]=\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Z_i^2], \qquad \mathop{\mathrm{sd}}[Y_i]=\sqrt{\mathop{\mathrm{\mathop{\mathrm{V}}}}[Y_i]}.

The standard deviation is in the response’s original units. Signed deviations can cancel even when the responses lie far from the center; squared distances cannot.

Independence: Definition and Equivalent Condition

Definition. Random variables Y and Z are independent if \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[f(Y)g(Z)] = \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[f(Y)]\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[g(Z)] \qqtext{for all functions $f$ and $g$} Here we work with random variables taking finitely many values.

Equivalent. Their joint distribution is the product of their marginal distributions, P(Y=y,Z=z)=P(Y=y)P(Z=z). Knowing the value of one then does not change the probabilities for the other.

One Uniform Draw

Sample uniformly with replacement from y_1\ldots y_m, with population mean \mu and variance \sigma^2. Recall the calculation for a function of a uniform draw: \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[f(Y_i)]=\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^m f(y_j). Choosing f(y)=(y-\mu)^2 also identifies the population variance, \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[(Y_i-\mu)^2]=\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^m(y_j-\mu)^2=\sigma^2.

Centered Products

Return to the centered responses in the Z picture. Linearity implies \begin{aligned} \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Z_i] &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{definition of the centered response}}{=}\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Y_i-\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Y_i]]\\ &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{linearity}}{=}\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Y_i]-\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Y_i]=0. \end{aligned} For distinct draws i\ne j, independence implies \begin{aligned} \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[\textcolor{#EF476F}{Z_i}\textcolor{#118AB2}{Z_j}] &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{independence}}{=}\textcolor{#EF476F}{\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Z_i]}\textcolor{#118AB2}{\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Z_j]}\\ &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{centering.}}{=}0 \end{aligned}

When i=j, the product is a square: \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Z_i^2]=\mathop{\mathrm{\mathop{\mathrm{V}}}}[Y_i]=\sigma^2. These are the two cases we will use when we square the sample mean.

The Variance of the Sample Mean

Because \hat\mu-\mu=n^{-1}\sum_i Z_i, we can now calculate its variance.1 \begin{aligned} \mathop{\mathrm{\mathop{\mathrm{V}}}}[\hat\mu] &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{center}}{=}\mathop{\mathrm{E}}\left[\left(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n Z_i\right)^2\right]\\ &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{square}}{=}\frac1{n^2}\mathop{\mathrm{E}}\left[\textcolor{#EF476F}{\sum_{i=1}^n Z_i}\textcolor{#118AB2}{\sum_{j=1}^n Z_j}\right]\\ &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{linearity}}{=}\frac1{n^2}\textcolor{#EF476F}{\sum_{i=1}^n}\textcolor{#118AB2}{\sum_{j=1}^n}\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[\textcolor{#EF476F}{Z_i}\textcolor{#118AB2}{Z_j}]\\ &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{distinct-index terms vanish}}{=}\frac1{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Z_i^2]\\ &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{equal one-draw variances}}{=}\frac{n\sigma^2}{n^2}=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}. \end{aligned}

Conclusion

The variance of our mean is 1/n times the variance of one observation.

\mathop{\mathrm{\mathop{\mathrm{V}}}}\sb*{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i} = \frac{\mathop{\mathrm{\mathop{\mathrm{V}}}}[Y_1]}{n}

So the standard deviation of our mean is 1/\sqrt{n} times the standard deviation of one observation.

\mathop{\mathrm{sd}}\sb*{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i} = \frac{\mathop{\mathrm{sd}}[Y_1]}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}

Binary Variance

For a binary Y that equals one with probability \theta, its mean is \theta, and direct substitution into the definition implies \mathop{\mathrm{\mathop{\mathrm{V}}}}[Y] =\theta(1-\theta)^2+(1-\theta)\theta^2 =\theta(1-\theta).

For a binary population, \mu=\theta and \sigma^2=\theta(1-\theta).

A Binary Application

This explains a picture we already know. The three Binomial sampling distributions below have the same sample size and success probabilities \theta=.68,.70,.72. Their centers move with \theta; their widths are governed by \sqrt{\theta(1-\theta)/n}.

The previous homework computed the corresponding calibrated widths as functions of \theta for n=100 and n=625. The variance formula explains both patterns: precision is worst near \theta=1/2, where \theta(1-\theta) is largest, and multiplying the sample size by 6.25 divides the standard deviation by \sqrt{6.25}=2.5.

Homework Preview: Sampling without Replacement

When we sample without replacement, the calculation is almost the same. Centering, squaring, and linearity go through unchanged. The difference is in the distinct-index terms.

\mathop{\mathrm{\mathop{\mathrm{V}}}}[\hat\theta] = \mathop{\mathrm{\mathop{\mathrm{V}}}}\sb*{\frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n Y_i} = \frac{\theta(1-\theta)}{n} \times \frac{m-n}{m-1}

When j=i, we still get \mathop{\mathrm{\mathop{\mathrm{V}}}}[Y_i] = \theta(1-\theta).

When j \neq i, we can no longer factor the expectation—our observations aren’t independent. Instead, we get a small negative term. Why negative? Because if your first call reached a 1, there’s one fewer 1 in the population, so your second call is slightly less likely to reach a 1. This negative covariance is why sampling without replacement gives us better precision.

The cross-term we have already been calculating has a name. The covariance of X and Y is the mean of the product after centering both variables: \mathop{\mathrm{Cov}}(X,Y)=\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[(X-\mathop{\mathrm{E}}X)(Y-\mathop{\mathrm{E}}Y)]. That is the whole move: center and multiply. When we center and square out a sum, the diagonal terms are variances and the off-diagonal terms are covariances. Under independence the covariances vanish. Without replacement they need not vanish.

The covariance works out to \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[Z_iZ_j] = -\frac{\theta(1-\theta)}{m-1} when j \neq i. We’ll leave deriving this for homework.

With this in hand, we can finish the calculation. There are n diagonal terms (when j=i) and n(n-1) off-diagonal terms (when j \neq i).

\begin{aligned} \mathop{\mathrm{\mathop{\mathrm{V}}}}[\hat\theta] &= \frac{1}{n^2} \left\{ * \right\}{ n \cdot \theta(1-\theta) + n(n-1) \cdot \left\{ * \right\}{-\frac{\theta(1-\theta)}{m-1}} } \\ &= \frac{\theta(1-\theta)}{n} \left\{ * \right\}{1 - \frac{n-1}{m-1}} = \frac{\theta(1-\theta)}{n} \times \frac{m-n}{m-1} \end{aligned}

NoteVariance and Independent Copies

Variance has two equivalent forms that reveal different things. Let Y' be an independent copy of Y: it has the same distribution as Y and is independent of it. Then \mathop{\mathrm{\mathop{\mathrm{V}}}}[Y] =\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[(Y-\mathop{\mathrm{E}}Y)^2] =\frac12\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[(Y-Y')^2]. The first expression is the definition. The second says that variance measures how different two independent draws tend to be. The factor 1/2 appears because both draws vary: writing \mu=\mathop{\mathrm{E}}Y=\mathop{\mathrm{E}}Y' and expanding implies \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[(Y-Y')^2] =\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[(Y-\mu)^2]+\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[(Y'-\mu)^2] -2\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[(Y-\mu)(Y'-\mu)] =2\mathop{\mathrm{\mathop{\mathrm{V}}}}[Y], where independence makes the final cross-term zero.

We now know how to calculate two summaries without finding a random variable’s full distribution. Its mean tells us where it is centered. Its variance tells us how far two independent realizations tend to be from each other.

What can those two numbers guarantee if we know nothing about the distribution’s shape? More than you might expect. They cannot tell us the exact probability of every event, but they can limit how much probability can live far from the center. The standard deviation becomes a yardstick that works for every distribution with a finite variance.

Markov’s Inequality

Markov’s inequality says that for a non-negative random variable X and any t > 0, P(X \ge t) \le \frac{\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[X]}{t}.

The usual proof of Markov’s inequality is based on a few simple observations.

  1. The expectation of the indicator variable 1_{\ge t}(X) is the probability that X exceeds t, P(X \ge t).
  2. If we have some function of u that’s always larger than 1_{\ge t}, i.e., one satisfying u_t(x) \ge 1_{\ge t}(x) for all x, we know that \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[u_t(X)] \ge \mathop{\mathrm{E}}1_{\ge t}(X) for any random variable X. If it’s always larger for non-negative x, then \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[u_t(X)] \ge \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[1_{\ge t}(X)] for any non-negative random variable X.
  3. The function u_t(x)=x/t is such a function.2
ExerciseProving Markov’s Inequality

Prove Markov’s inequality.

SolutionSolution

To see that u_t(x) = x/t \ge 1_{\ge t}(x) for all x \ge 0, consider separately the cases x \ge t and x < t. If x \ge t, then 1_{\ge t}(x)=1 and x/t \ge 1. If x < t, then 1_{\ge t}(x)=0 and x/t \ge 0. So u_t(x) \ge 1_{\ge t}(x) for all non-negative x. It follows that for any non-negative random variable X, P(X \ge t) = \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[1_{\ge t}(X)] \le \mathop{\mathrm{E}}[u_t(X)] = \mathop{\mathrm{E}}\sb*{\frac{X}{t}} = \frac{\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[X]}{t}.

Chebyshev’s Inequality

Apply Markov’s inequality to the nonnegative random variable (X-\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[X])^2. For t>0, \begin{aligned} P(|X-\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[X]|\ge t) &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{Squaring preserves the comparison of nonnegative quantities.}}{=}P((X-\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[X])^2\ge t^2)\\ &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{Markov's inequality}}{\le}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[(X-\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[X])^2]}{t^2}\\ &\overset{\texttip{\small{\text{?}}}{Definition of variance}}{=}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{\mathop{\mathrm{V}}}}[X]}{t^2}. \end{aligned} This is Chebyshev’s inequality. It applies to every random variable with finite variance.

The Standard Deviation is Standard

Write \sigma=\mathop{\mathrm{sd}}(X). If \sigma>0, then for every k>0, Chebyshev’s inequality says P(|X-\mathop{\mathrm{E}}X|\ge k\sigma)\le \frac1{k^2}. If \sigma=0, then X=\mathop{\mathrm{E}}X with probability one, so there is no positive departure from the mean to bound. The statement contains no density, no named distribution, and no approximation. Two standard deviations means a tail probability no larger than 1/4 for every distribution with finite variance. Four standard deviations means no larger than 1/16. The exact tail probability changes from one distribution to another; the guarantee does not.

That is what earns the word standard. Once distance is measured in units of \sigma, the same number k carries the same universal probability guarantee everywhere. The guarantee can be loose, but it cannot be false merely because the distribution has an unfamiliar shape.

Two Moments Give You an Interval

Markov’s Inequality and Interval Estimation

So far, when we’ve calibrated interval estimates using our estimator’s standard deviation, we’ve relied on normal approximation. In effect, we’ve been using a formula for P(\lvert\hat\theta - \theta\rvert \le \epsilon) that’s accurate when \hat\theta has a normal distribution and close enough when its distribution is close enough to normal. In this problem, we’re going to think about doing without this reliance on approximate normality.

Let’s consider \hat\theta, an unbiased estimator of \theta with standard deviation \sigma, so the normal approximation to the distribution of \hat\theta-\theta has the density f_{0,\sigma}(x) below.

P\p*{|\hat\theta - \theta| \le \epsilon} \approx \int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon} f_{0,\sigma}(x) dx \qfor f_{0, \sigma}(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma} e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}

The reason we’ve been talking about interval estimators of the form \hat\theta \pm 1.96 \sigma is that, if this approximation were perfect, these interval estimators would have 95% coverage. That is, it’d be true that P(|\hat\theta-\theta| \le 1.96 \sigma) = .95. And if the approximation is pretty good, we should still expect coverage close to that. But suppose we’re not confident that it is. Markov’s inequality allows us to calibrate interval estimates in terms of our estimator’s standard deviation without any caveats about its sampling distribution being approximately normal. Let’s give it a shot.

Exercise 2  

Exercise

Let \hat\theta be an unbiased estimator of \theta with standard deviation \sigma. By applying Markov’s inequality to |\hat\theta-\theta|^2, find a lower bound on P(|\hat\theta-\theta| \le t\sigma) as a function of t. For what choice of t is this bound equal to .95? Explain how you can use this to get an interval estimate \hat\theta \pm t\sigma with a coverage probability of at least 95%.

TipFrom Upper Bounds to Lower Bounds.

If you have an upper bound on P(X > \epsilon), then you have a lower bound on P(X \le \epsilon). P(X \le \epsilon)=1-P(X > \epsilon) because X > \epsilon if and only if it is not the case that X \le \epsilon, so if P(X > \epsilon) \le u, then P(X \le \epsilon) = 1-P(X > \epsilon) \ge 1-u. And if you’re troubled that Markov’s inequality says \ge instead of >, don’t be. You have a bound on P(X > \epsilon) too. P(X > \epsilon) \le P(X\ge \epsilon) because X \ge \epsilon whenever X > \epsilon.

SolutionSolution

For t>0 and \sigma>0, apply Markov’s inequality to |\hat\theta-\theta|^2, which has mean-square \sigma^2 because \hat\theta is unbiased:

P(|\hat\theta-\theta|^2 \ge (t\sigma)^2) \le \frac{\mathop{\mathrm{E}}[(\hat\theta-\theta)^2]}{(t\sigma)^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{(t\sigma)^2} = \frac{1}{t^2}. The probability that |\hat\theta-\theta| > t\sigma is also no larger than 1/t^2 because this happens only if |\hat\theta-\theta|^2 \ge (t\sigma)^2. And it follows that the probability that |\hat\theta-\theta| \le t\sigma is at least 1-1/t^2.

To find the value of t that makes this lower bound equal to .95, we solve 1-1/t^2=.95 to find t=\sqrt{1/(1-.95)}= \sqrt{20} \approx 4.5. And because \theta \in [\hat\theta - t\sigma, \hat\theta + t\sigma] if (and only if) |\hat\theta-\theta| \le t\sigma, it follows that this interval has a coverage probability of at least .95.

Transition: Honest but Loose

The guaranteed 95% interval reaches roughly 4.5 standard deviations in both directions. The exact Binomial calculation students already know usually gives a much narrower interval. That comparison creates the next question: can we keep the cheap mean-and-standard-deviation calculation without paying for a uselessly wide universal guarantee?

Normal approximation answers yes. It keeps the same two moments and adds information about shape. The resulting interval is approximate rather than universal, but it is close to the sharp exact Binomial interval. The next chapter puts all three answers beside each other: exact, guaranteed, and approximate.
